## Introduction

NestJS is a long-running Node server, so it's happiest where it can stay warm and connect to a database with low latency. Here's where to put it.

Below I walk through each option, what actually sets them apart, and which one I would reach for depending on your app.

NestJS has a particular shape that should drive your hosting choice. It is a structured, long-lived application server. It boots once, wires up its dependency-injection container, opens a pool of database connections, and then serves requests for days or weeks without restarting. That model rewards hosts that keep a process warm and penalises hosts that tear it down between requests. It also means the database relationship matters enormously: a NestJS app that has to re-establish connections or reach across a slow network to its database will feel sluggish no matter how fast the server is. The best homes for Nest keep the process alive, sit close to the database, and give you health checks and graceful restarts so deploys do not drop requests.

## How we compared

We judged each option by how it handles a real NestJS API over time, not just the first deploy but the hundredth:

- **Process model.** Does it keep Nest warm, or fight its long-lived nature?
- **Database proximity.** How easy it is to colocate Postgres or MySQL for low latency.
- **Deploy ergonomics:** health checks, zero-downtime rollouts, and rollback.
- **Cost trajectory.** What it costs at launch versus at steady production scale.

## What to look for

Before comparing names, get clear on your own priorities. The factors that matter most here:

- **Fit for your stack.** The best tool is the one that matches how you already work.
- **Pricing and value.** Weigh the real cost against the time it saves you.
- **Developer experience:** good docs and a clean API compound every single day.
- **Scalability.** Will it still fit when your project (or team) grows?

One more Nest-specific consideration: background work. NestJS apps frequently run scheduled tasks, queue consumers, and WebSocket gateways alongside the HTTP server. Hosts that only understand request/response (classic serverless) make those awkward; hosts that run a persistent container handle them naturally. Factor in what your app does besides answer HTTP requests before you commit.

## The top picks

### Railway

Push-to-deploy simplicity with a database attached in minutes, with great DX for NestJS.

Railway is the fastest way to get a NestJS API and its database live together. Connect a repo, and it detects the Node app, builds it, and deploys it; add a Postgres or Redis service from the same dashboard and it is wired up with environment variables in minutes. For a solo developer or a small team that wants to spend their energy on the API rather than on infrastructure, the DX is delightful. The trade-off is cost at scale: usage-based pricing that is a bargain for small and medium apps can climb as you grow, so it is worth modelling your steady-state usage rather than extrapolating from the generous early experience.

### Render

Managed web services with health checks, autoscaling, and zero-downtime deploys.

Render is the grown-up managed platform in this list. It treats your NestJS app as a proper long-running web service: configurable health check endpoints, zero-downtime deploys, horizontal autoscaling, and managed Postgres and Redis in the same region for low-latency database access. It sits comfortably between Railway's hobby-friendly simplicity and the full control of a VPS: more production-oriented than the former, and far less work than the latter. If you want managed infrastructure that behaves like a real deployment platform without running servers yourself, Render is the safe, boring-in-a-good-way choice.

<AffBox id="render" />

### Vercel (Fluid Compute)

Runs NestJS natively now, which helps if your frontend already lives there.

Historically Vercel was a poor fit for a long-lived server like Nest, but Fluid Compute changed the calculus. It keeps functions warm, supports streaming, and can run a genuine NestJS backend rather than forcing you to contort it into stateless handlers. The compelling case is consolidation: if your Next.js or React frontend already lives on Vercel, putting the Nest API there too gives you one platform, one deploy pipeline, and preview environments that cover the whole stack. Watch the usage-based billing for heavy or sustained workloads, and confirm your background jobs and WebSocket needs fit the model, but for frontend-colocated APIs it is now a real option.

### Fly.io

Run your Nest container close to users globally with persistent volumes.

Fly.io runs your Docker container as a lightweight VM, optionally in multiple regions, so your NestJS API sits physically near your users worldwide. It supports persistent volumes and has first-class Postgres options, which suits stateful needs and global latency-sensitive APIs. The model is more hands-on than Railway or Render (you work with a Dockerfile and Fly's config), but far less work than managing a raw VPS. Choose it when global distribution of your API is a real requirement rather than a nice-to-have, and you are comfortable thinking in containers.

### VPS (Hetzner/DO) + Docker

Cheapest at scale and fully in your control.

A Dockerised NestJS app on a Hetzner or DigitalOcean VPS is the most economical option once your traffic is steady and predictable, and it gives you total control over the runtime, the database placement, and the data itself. The cost is ownership: you set up the container, the reverse proxy, TLS, process supervision, and backups yourself. For a team comfortable with ops, that one-time setup buys a flat, low monthly bill that does not move with traffic, the opposite of the usage-metered platforms. It is the natural destination for a mature API whose costs have grown large enough to justify the operational investment.

## A production-ready Dockerfile

Whichever container host you choose, a lean multi-stage build keeps your NestJS image small and your boots fast:

```dockerfile
# Build stage
FROM node:22-alpine AS build
WORKDIR /app
COPY package*.json ./
RUN npm ci
COPY . .
RUN npm run build

# Runtime stage
FROM node:22-alpine
WORKDIR /app
COPY package*.json ./
RUN npm ci --omit=dev
COPY --from=build /app/dist ./dist
EXPOSE 3000
CMD ["node", "dist/main.js"]
```

Pair that with a health check endpoint (`@nestjs/terminus` makes this trivial) so your platform knows when the app is actually ready to receive traffic, and your zero-downtime deploys will behave.

## Common mistakes

The most frequent mistake with NestJS hosting is deploying the API far from its database. Colocate them in the same region or the connection latency will dominate your response times. The second is skipping health checks, which turns every deploy into a gamble that drops requests during the restart window. The third is forcing Nest's background jobs and WebSocket gateways onto a pure request/response serverless model that cannot keep a process alive between invocations; if your app does persistent work, pick a host that runs a persistent container. Finally, do not forget connection-pool sizing: too many instances each opening a large pool can exhaust your database's connection limit.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which should I choose if I'm just starting out?**
Start with the option that has the gentlest learning curve and best documentation, and you can always switch once you understand your needs better.

**Is the most popular option always the best?**
No. Popularity means more tutorials and community help, but the *right* choice depends on your specific stack, budget, and goals.

**Can I run NestJS serverless?**
You can, and Vercel's Fluid Compute makes it far more viable than classic serverless did by keeping functions warm. Just confirm that your scheduled tasks, queue consumers, and WebSocket gateways fit the model, since a persistent container host handles those more naturally.

**What database should I run alongside it?**
Postgres is the default for most NestJS apps, and every platform here offers it managed and colocatable. The important thing is proximity and a sensibly sized connection pool, not the brand.

## Final verdict

Railway or Render get a NestJS API live with the least friction; a Dockerised VPS is the most economical once you're comfortable with ops.

Start on Railway for the fastest path from repo to running API with a database attached, or Render when you want managed infrastructure that behaves like a real production platform. Reach for Fly.io when global latency is a genuine requirement, consider Vercel if your frontend already lives there, and graduate to a Dockerised VPS when steady traffic makes the flat bill worth the operational investment. All five will keep a NestJS process warm; the decision is about how much ops you want to own and where your users and data live.
